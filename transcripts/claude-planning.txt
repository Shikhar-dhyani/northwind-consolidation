what are the knowledge i should acquire about accounting that needs to take decisions based for these assignment

For this assignment you need a fairly narrow slice of accounting: about seven topics, all in group consolidation. Below, each topic comes with why it matters here and the decision it drives in your build.

1. Double-entry basics (must know)
Debits and credits. Every entry has equal debits and credits. Assets and expenses normally sit on the debit side. Liabilities, equity and revenue sit on the credit side.
Trial balance (TB). A list of every account’s balance. It must net to zero.
How the P&L feeds the balance sheet. Revenue minus expenses gives profit, and profit flows into retained earnings.
Why it matters here: the three TBs use three sign formats.
The US file has separate debit and credit columns.
The UK file has one signed balance, where credits are negative.
The India file has an amount plus a Dr/Cr flag.
Your first step is converting all three to one sign convention, then checking each TB nets to zero.
2. The three statements and how they link
Balance sheet: assets = liabilities + equity.
P&L: revenue minus expenses gives profit for the year.
Changes in equity: opening equity + profit + other movements = closing equity. One of those other movements is the translation difference.
Why it matters here: your outputs have to tie together. The closing equity in the changes-in-equity statement must equal equity on the balance sheet. The opening figures come from prior_year_group.csv.
3. Foreign currency (IAS 21): the hardest part, learn it well

There are two different operations here, and mixing them up is the most common mistake.

A. Retranslating foreign-currency items inside one entity’s own books (policy 3)

Monetary items are cash, receivables, payables and loans. When one is in a foreign currency, revalue it at the closing rate. The gain or loss goes to the P&L (account 7300).
Non-monetary items (inventory, PPE, equity) stay at their historical rate.
In this assignment:
The UK’s USD payable to the US is still at old rates.
The UK’s SGD bank account needs a closing GBP/SGD rate, which isn’t provided.

B. Translating a whole entity’s statements into GBP (policy 2)

Assets and liabilities use the closing rate.
Income and expenses use the average rate. For the US that’s the FY2024 average. For India it’s the H2 average, because India only joined the group on 1 July.
Equity uses historical rates. For India, that’s the 1 July spot rate.
The difference that’s left over is the translation reserve (account 3300). It isn’t a figure you look up. It’s what forces the translated balance sheet to balance.
Easy to miss: the US parent is USD-functional, so it gets translated too, not just India.

Rate mechanics

The rates are quoted as “USD per 1 GBP”, so GBP = USD ÷ 1.2530.
For INR there’s no direct GBP rate. Policy 2 says to multiply the two given rates of the same type: closing GBP/INR = 1.2530 × 85.62.
4. Consolidation basics (IFRS 10)
Add everything up line by line. Combine all three entities’ assets, liabilities, income and expenses, as if they were one company.
Eliminate the parent’s investment. Remove the parent’s “Investment in subsidiary” against the subsidiary’s equity at acquisition. You don’t count the same money twice.
Include profits only from the acquisition date. Only India’s July–December P&L goes into group profit. Its April–June profit (INR 18m) is pre-acquisition and becomes part of the net assets bought.
Order of operations, the key design insight:
Make entity-level fixes in local currency: journals, mapping corrections, retranslation.
Translate each entity into GBP.
Eliminate intercompany balances and the investments.
Split out NCI.
Produce the statements.
5. Acquisitions, goodwill and NCI (IFRS 3)
Goodwill = consideration paid + NCI − fair value of net assets acquired.
For India: 690m + (20% × 678m) − 678m = INR 147.6m.
NCI measured at its “proportionate share” (policy 4). NCI starts at 20% of net assets, with no goodwill allocated to it.
What NCI gets afterwards:
20% of India’s post-acquisition profit
20% of India’s translation difference, which is often missed
The other 80% of the translation difference goes to the translation reserve.
Goodwill is held in INR (policy 4). You retranslate it at the closing rate each year, which creates a translation difference of its own. Under the proportionate method, that belongs entirely to the owners.
UK investment issue: the US paid USD 1.27m for the UK in 2016. To eliminate that investment you’d need the UK’s equity at that date, which isn’t provided. That’s a strong clarifying question.
6. Intercompany eliminations
Remove both sides. The US receivable cancels against the UK payable, and the US fee income cancels against the UK fee expense.
Reconcile first. In practice the two sides rarely agree. Typical causes:
Items in transit: the US booked a USD 10,160 invoice that the UK hasn’t recorded. Policy 5 says the receiving entity records it at the closing rate.
FX timing: each side booked at different rates.
Genuine errors.
Leftover FX goes to 7300. Policy 5 sends any exchange difference that remains on income and expense to that account.
Why it matters here: the intercompany_difference control figure asks you to split the gap into these causes.
7. Controls and journal validation
Sub-ledger reconciliation. The debtors and creditors control accounts should match the sub-ledger totals.
The UK is off by 45,000 on both.
Decide whether to flag it or adjust it. Flagging is safer than guessing.
Manual journal checks: balanced? In the right period? Do the accounts exist and map? Duplicate? Would it leave a nonsensical balance?
Three outcomes: posted, rejected, or held for review. You’ll need a clear rule for each.
Period cut-off. India’s year runs April to March, so its TB is year-to-date for April–December. You use the monthly file to split out the pre-acquisition months.
What you can skip

Cash flow statements (they’re not required, despite the column in the chart of accounts), deferred tax, fair value adjustments (the note says there are none), impairment testing, and the full-goodwill method for NCI.

whats is this gbp and sgd and everything i need to understand and take decision

GBP, USD, INR and SGD are currency codes. Every currency decision in this assignment comes down to two questions: which exchange rate do I use, and where does the difference go? The examples below use the real numbers from your data.

1. The currencies
Code	Currency	Who uses it here
GBP	British pound (£)	UK subsidiary, and the group reports in GBP
USD	US dollar ($)	US parent
INR	Indian rupee (₹)	India subsidiary
SGD	Singapore dollar	Only one item: the UK company’s bank account at DBS (a Singapore bank)
2. Two key terms
Functional currency is the currency a company does its business and keeps its books in. US books are in USD, UK in GBP, India in INR.
Presentation currency is the currency the group’s final reports are shown in. Here that’s GBP.

So everything eventually has to be converted into GBP.

3. Reading the exchange rates

From fx_rates.csv:

GBP,USD,closing,1.2530   → £1 = $1.2530
USD,INR,closing,85.62    → $1 = ₹85.62

To convert into GBP, divide:

$18,400,000 US revenue ÷ 1.2780 (average rate) = £14,397,496.09

There’s no direct GBP/INR rate, so policy 2 says to multiply the two given rates of the same type:

Closing: 1.2530 × 85.62 = ₹107.28 per £1
4. Rate types: which one, when

A rate is a snapshot of one moment, and rates move every day. The file gives you several:

Rate	Date	Used for
Opening	31 Dec 2023	Start of year (mostly context)
Average	Full year 2024	US income and expenses (they happened throughout the year)
H2 average	Jul–Dec 2024	India income and expenses (India only joined on 1 July)
Spot	1 Jul 2024	India acquisition date: the investment, goodwill and India’s equity at that point
Closing	31 Dec 2024	All assets and liabilities on the year-end balance sheet

The logic: what you own or owe uses today’s rate (closing). What you earned or spent over a period uses that period’s average. Equity stays at the rate on the day it arose (historical).

5. The two conversion jobs (don’t mix them up)
Job A: retranslation inside one company’s own books (policy 3)

A company can hold money or debts in a currency that isn’t its own. Those items get revalued at the closing rate, and the gain or loss goes to the P&L, account 7300.

Example 1: the UK owes the US $629,840

The UK booked it at £490,707.72, using the rates on each invoice date.
At the closing rate: 629,840 ÷ 1.2530 = £502,665.60.
The debt grew by £11,957.88, which is an FX loss for the UK.
There’s also a $10,160 invoice the UK hasn’t booked yet (in transit). At the closing rate that’s £8,108.54.

Example 2: the UK’s SGD bank account

It holds SGD 40,000, booked at £23,010.
That implies a rate of about 1.738 SGD per £, which doesn’t match any rate in the file.
To revalue it you need a closing GBP/SGD rate. The file has only opening and average rates.
You can’t cross through USD either, because no USD/SGD rate is given.
Job B: translating a whole company into GBP (policy 2)

Each company’s full trial balance is converted into GBP:

Assets and liabilities use the closing rate.
Income and expenses use the average rate (H2 average for India).
Equity uses the historical rate (for India, the 1 July spot rate).

The UK is already in GBP, so it needs no translation. The US and India both do. People often forget the US.

6. The translation reserve

Because different lines use different rates, the translated balance sheet won’t balance on its own. The gap is the translation reserve (account 3300).

Intuition, using India:

India’s net assets at acquisition were ₹678m.
At the spot rate on 1 July (₹105.45 per £) that’s £6,429,791.
At the closing rate on 31 Dec (₹107.28 per £), the same rupees are worth £6,319,801.
The rupee weakened, so the same rupees are now worth £109,990 less.

That loss isn’t a business loss; nothing was sold. So it doesn’t go to the P&L. It goes into the translation reserve in equity.

India is 80% owned, so 80% goes to the translation reserve and 20% to NCI (the minority owners).

Goodwill works the same way. ₹147.6m is £1,399,760 at the spot rate but £1,375,815 at the closing rate. That difference also goes to the reserve, all of it to the owners.

7. Decisions you have to make
The SGD bank balance (sgd_bank_gbp): the closing rate is missing.
Option (a): null with a reason. This matches their rule “an honest null scores better than a confident guess”. I’d recommend it.
Option (b): use the average 1.7120 (£23,364.49). That breaks policy 3, which requires the closing rate.
Option (c): keep the booked £23,010. The rate behind it is unknown.
Use one of your 3 clarifying questions on this.
What happens to totals that include the SGD balance? It sits inside total assets and total equity. You can either:
report those totals as null, or
include £23,010 at book value and state that in issues.json.
Pick one, record it in DECISIONS.md, and apply it consistently.
The UK’s USD payable: book the £11,957.88 retranslation loss to 7300 before eliminating intercompany balances. Do this at entity level first, then translate.
The in-transit invoice: record it in the UK books at the closing rate (policy 5), then eliminate.
Rate lookup in code: write one function that returns a rate by currency pair and type, computes cross rates, and fails loudly if a rate is missing. It should never fall back silently to another rate type. That’s exactly the failure mode they’re testing for.
